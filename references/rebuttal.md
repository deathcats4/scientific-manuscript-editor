# Rebuttal and journal correspondence

Use for reviewer responses, decision-letter replies, revision summaries to
the editor, and cover letters. The revised manuscript is the scientific
deliverable; the letter is its index and record. Load
[continuity-and-consistency.md](continuity-and-consistency.md) when the
revision spans sections or a long history, and [synthesis.md](synthesis.md)
for cover-letter compression.

## Scientific function

A response letter turns review into a decided, checkable record: every
comment is answered, every answer states what changed in the manuscript and
where, and every disagreement gives its reason. The letter should let the
editor and reviewers verify the revision without re-reviewing the whole
manuscript, and let the author see exactly which positions were conceded,
clarified, or defended.

## Author control is stricter here

Every concession of a scientific point belongs to the author. Classify each
comment before drafting:

- **Factual correction** — an error in the manuscript. Draft the correction
  directly; the supplied record and revised text are the basis.
- **Clarification** — the manuscript already supports the answer. Draft it
  directly from existing content; add new text only as flagged proposals
  until the author accepts it.
- **New analysis or experiment** — the entrypoint's material sufficiency
  gate applies: never present an unrun analysis as run. Draft the response
  only after the author supplies the result, authorizes the work, or accepts
  a stated refusal.
- **Scientific disagreement** — the author decides the position. Draft the
  disagreement, its evidence, and any change made anyway only after the
  author accepts that position.

The letter never concedes, apologizes for, or reinterprets the science on
the author's behalf, and never thanks where no genuine change or useful
point exists. Gratitude is specific or absent.

## Point-by-point structure

Quote each comment verbatim and in full; a paraphrase that weakens or
narrows a comment is an integrity failure. Answer under each quote in the
order the reviewer used, unless a global response genuinely covers several
identical comments—then answer once, say where it applies, and reference it
from each instance. State each response as the decision, the action taken,
and the location of the change (section, page, line); quote the new or
revised manuscript text when it is short, and locate it when it is long.
Keep responses to unrelated comments independent, and mark author decisions
still pending rather than smoothing them into confident prose.

When a comment rests on a misunderstanding, check first whether the
manuscript caused it. If it did, fix the text and thank the reviewer for
exposing the ambiguity; if it did not, state the misunderstanding plainly
and answer the comment as written.

## Tone and length

Match force to content. A short factual answer stays short; a contested
interpretation gets its full evidence and reasoning, stated once. Do not
build defensive walls—repeated apologies, preemptive rebuttals of comments
not made, or ceremonial restatements of the reviewer's point. Politeness is
in precision and completeness, not in intensifiers. Use the correspondence
language the venue expects; quoted manuscript text keeps its own language.

## Cover letters

A cover letter states in a few sentences what the paper establishes, why it
fits the journal's scope, and any factual constraints the editor needs
(prior submissions, related manuscripts, data or funding conditions). Use
the synthesis reference's compression rules; claim exactly what the
manuscript earns, and do not summarize results the paper does not contain.

## Completion check

Every comment has a response or an explicit, author-accepted deferral; every
change the letter claims exists in the revised manuscript and matches its
description; quoted comments match the decision letter verbatim; no response
commits the author to a position the author has not accepted.

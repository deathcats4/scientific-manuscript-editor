# Evidence and citations

## Evidence levels

- **Observation**: what the data, image, experiment, or table directly shows.
- **Interpretation**: what that pattern may indicate or what process it fits.
- **Implication**: what the interpretation means for the research question,
  system, or broader process.

Keep these levels legible in sentence order and verb choice. A plausible
interpretation can be stated directly when the supplied evidence supports it;
do not automatically weaken every claim to `may` or `might`.

## Inferential bridges

Concise prose must still show the reasoning a reader needs to move from an
observation to an interpretation. Make an intermediate step explicit when the
conclusion is not contained in the observation itself, when evidence types are
being combined, when the passage moves from a local pattern to a broader
process, or when it advances from association to a causal or mechanistic claim.
Do not replace this reasoning with a bare transition or a calibrated verb.

Expand the scientific bridge, not the editor's private deliberation. The paper
needs to show why the supplied evidence supports the author's interpretation;
it does not need to show every alternative the editor considered.

## Claim calibration

Choose verbs by evidence, study design, and scope. Use `demonstrates`, `confirms`,
or `proves` only when the design and evidence justify that level. Use
`supports`, `indicates`, `reflects`, or `is consistent with` when they accurately
describe the relationship. Correlation, model fit, prediction, simulation, and
absence of detected difference each have narrower meanings than causation,
mechanism, proof, or equivalence.

### Claim-first evidential discipline

Scientific caution should be carried primarily by the claim itself. Calibrate
its subject, represented population or object, temporal and spatial range,
causal force, and degree of certainty to what the evidence supports. State that
bounded claim directly.

Before adding a caveat, ask whether the same evidential boundary can be enforced
by narrowing the claim or choosing a more accurate verb. If it can, revise the
claim and omit the caveat. A broader interpretation that would be uncertain is
not a reason to qualify a narrower interpretation the manuscript never makes.

Use an explicit limitation when omitting it would materially mislead the reader
in at least one of these ways:

1. The reader could mistake what was measured for a different population,
   object, time interval, spatial scale, or type of evidence.
2. The limitation could change the direction or identity of the scientific
   interpretation, not merely reduce confidence in it.
3. The sentence crosses a substantive inferential level that the available
   evidence cannot support through claim calibration alone; add the missing
   evidence, reduce the claim, or state the unresolved boundary.

Generic truths such as imperfect data, finite samples, modern conditions not
necessarily equalling past conditions, association not proving causation,
non-unique sources, or unexcluded alternatives do not automatically belong in
the manuscript. Include one only when the reader needs it to understand the
claim actually being made.

## Source status

Call a citation `verified` only after inspecting enough of the source to confirm
that it supports the specific statement. Otherwise use `support not verified`
and tell the author what needs checking. Never create a citation, DOI, number,
or mechanism from memory or a title alone.

## Citation fit

Check the fit between each important citation and the claim it follows. Identify
whether the source is being used for background, a method, an observation, a
mechanism, a comparison, or an interpretive aid. Confirm that the cited study's
object, conditions, scale, time range, and evidence type match the statement's
scope. A source can support a related fact without supporting the stronger
interpretation attached to it.

Keep the author's inference distinguishable from what the cited authors
reported. Do not add a citation only to make a sentence sound authoritative,
and do not use several citations as a substitute for explaining the evidence
chain. If a citation appears mismatched, missing, or too weak for the claim,
report the finding and the available options; do not silently replace the
reference or weaken the claim without the author's decision.

## Author decision rule for competing mechanisms

The manuscript does not need to enumerate every mechanism that could exist in
the field. A cited paper with a different mechanism is normally background,
parallel evidence, or a study of a different system. The task is to state the
explanation supported by the current evidence, not to prove that it is the only
possible explanation.

Apply this rule whenever a source, reviewer comment, or project record raises
another mechanism. Treat it as a competing interpretation only when it concerns
the same core phenomenon under comparable conditions, can plausibly explain
the supplied observations, and would change the central interpretation, causal
claim, scope, or conclusion. This is an author decision, not an automatic
rewriting decision. When that threshold may be met, pause the affected rewrite
and ask the author whether and how the comparison should enter the manuscript.
Before the author answers, report the source of the possible conflict and the
decision it would affect; do not add a rebuttal, invent an exclusion, or weaken
the current explanation by reflex.

## Evidence-chain check

For an important mechanism, use independent evidence types where available:
group or system patterns, local or microscopic observations, time-series or
tracer measurements, structural or process observations, and relevant context.
Do not turn this check into a requirement to list alternative explanations. Only
raise a competing mechanism through the author decision rule above.

## Evidence-opportunity scan

An author may have supplied evidence that is relevant to a claim but has not
yet connected it to the paragraph. For drafting, substantive rewriting, logic
repair, and interpretation-bearing Discussion work, scan the supplied
manuscript material before writing. Check the results, figures, tables,
captions, supplementary displays when supplied, and source pointers that could
bear on the active claim. The scan is a search for overlooked support, not a
license to expand the claim.

Classify each candidate:

- **Direct support** — the display or result measures the claimed object or
  relationship at the needed scope.
- **Indirect support** — it supports an intermediate link in the inference
  chain, but not the final interpretation by itself.
- **Partial support** — it supports only part of the claim, a narrower scope,
  or a weaker causal verb.
- **Needs analysis** — the pattern may be relevant, but it has not been
  analysed or verified in the supplied material.
- **Not support** — it is background, a different object or condition, or too
  weak to support the proposed statement.

Report the candidate's location, the observation it contains, the claim or
inference it may support, and the classification. For example:

```text
Potentially unused support:
- Fig. 3b: alteration zoning; indirect support for fluid involvement in U
  redistribution, but not by itself for a controlling mechanism.
- Table 2: element-ratio shift; partial support for the stated trend.
- Fig. 5: isotope pattern; needs analysis before it can enter the argument.
```

The author decides whether to incorporate a candidate and how strongly to state
it. Do not silently add a new figure reference, reinterpret an existing result,
promote an unanalysed pattern to evidence, or turn several correlated displays
into a causal claim. If the relevant display or source is unavailable, report
`support not verified` rather than inferring what it contains.
